1 · The problem

We have healthy data from our machines, and fault data only from other machines

A normal fault detector learns from examples of both healthy and broken machines. We cannot do that, because our turbomachines have never broken while we were recording. Public datasets do have broken machines, but they are different machines with different sensors.

We used CWRU and Paderborn as a test. We pretended Paderborn was "your machine": we hid its fault labels, used only its healthy data, and then checked if we could find its real faults.

2 · What we have done

Four experiments, from August to September 2026

  1. 20 AugExperiment 1

    Learn "normal" on one machine worked

    Idea
    Train an autoencoder (a network that copies its input) on healthy vibration only. If it copies a new signal badly, the signal is unusual.
    Result
    On CWRU it found 100% of faults (ROC-AUC 1.0).
    Lesson
    On the same machine, learning "normal" works. But CWRU is an easy lab dataset, so do not expect this score on real machines.
  2. Late AugExperiment 2

    Combine many sensors (hydraulic rig) partly

    Idea
    One healthy-only detector per component, using 17 sensors at 3 sampling rates.
    Result
    The cooler was detected very well. Valve, pump and accumulator were weaker (ROC-AUC 0.66 to 0.89). The pump found only 24% of faults at an acceptable false-alarm rate.
    Lesson
    Some faults are easy and some are not. Always set the threshold by the false-alarm rate you can accept, never by a score that looks good on paper.
  3. 10–17 SepExperiment 3

    Move an AI model from one machine to another failed

    Idea
    Train a spectrogram network on CWRU, adapt it with Paderborn healthy data (DANN, domain adaptation), then detect Paderborn faults.
    Result
    A first run looked good (0.80), but it did not reproduce. In 11 clean runs, the model called every Paderborn window faulty. Score: 0.50, which is chance. Training in the opposite direction also failed. DANN made things worse.
    Lesson
    The network learned how each test rig sounds, not what a fault looks like. That knowledge does not move to a new machine.
  4. 17–18 SepExperiment 4

    Use the physics of the fault instead worked

    Idea
    Measure 13 "envelope" features at each bearing's own fault frequencies, computed from its geometry. Train on CWRU. Set the alarm level from Paderborn healthy data only.
    Result
    At 900 rpm: 75% of faults found at 10% false alarms (score 0.82). Every faulty bearing was caught at least sometimes. At 1500 rpm the score dropped to 0.69, and we do not know why yet. Telling inner-race from outer-race faults worked only weakly (61%, chance is 50%).
    Lesson
    Features built from physics move between machines. This is the first cross-machine result that clearly beats chance.

4 · Why physics worked

A bearing fault is a repeated hit, and the spacing of the hits is the clue

Each time a ball rolls over a crack, it hits it. The hit makes the machine ring, like a bell. So a faulty bearing makes short bursts of ringing.

  • The ringing tone depends on the machine: its housing, shaft and mounting. It is different on every rig.
  • The time between hits depends only on the bearing's size and the shaft speed. Physics gives it to us. For example, for an outer-race crack it is called BPFO.

The learned network mostly listened to the ringing tone, which is the machine. The envelope method removes the tone and keeps only the rhythm of the hits, which is the fault.

The main lesson: teach the computer the physics of the fault, not the sound of the machine.

Anything that depends on the machine's own sound has to be learned from that machine's own data. Anything that comes from physics can move from one machine to another.

5 · The paths

Six layers, each needing only healthy data, geometry and speed

No single method is enough. A good system stacks several layers, from simple and safe to clever and new. You have all the data that every layer needs: raw vibration, speed and load, geometry, and several identical units.

L0

Standard severity limits ready now

ISO 20816 gives vibration velocity limits (mm/s RMS) by machine class: zones A (new) to D (damage likely). No data or training needed.

NeedsMachine class, velocity RMS
CatchesBig, late faults; a safety net
EffortDays
L1

Learn "normal" per unit and per operating state proven in-domain

This is Experiment 1, made aware of speed and load. Model how each feature normally changes with speed and load, then alarm on what the model cannot explain. Without this step, a speed change looks like a fault.

NeedsHealthy data across all speeds/loads
CatchesAny change from normal, but not its cause
EffortWeeks
L2

Physics features per component proven across machines

This is Experiment 4, extended to every part of a turbomachine. Each part has fault frequencies you can calculate from geometry and speed (see the table below). Watch the energy at those frequencies.

NeedsGeometry, speed signal, raw vibration
CatchesSpecific faults, with a likely cause
EffortWeeks per component type
L3

Fake faults inside real healthy data next experiment

Take real healthy recordings of your machine and add a simulated fault (hits at the right fault frequency). Then train a detector on "real healthy" against "healthy plus fake fault". The machine's own sound stays in both, so the detector can only learn the fault. This is untested here, which is why it is the next experiment.

NeedsHealthy data, geometry
CatchesFaults you can simulate; can also tell types apart
EffortExperiment: days
L4

Compare identical units with each other fits your fleet

If five identical units run at the same speed and load and one looks different, check that one. We saw that false alarms come from specific units (K002, K005 in Paderborn), not at random. So each unit needs its own baseline, and the fleet gives a second opinion.

NeedsSeveral identical units, same operating point
CatchesOne unit drifting away from its siblings
EffortDays, once L1/L2 features exist
L5

Watch the trend over time fits any machine

Real damage grows slowly. A feature that rises steadily for weeks says more than one bad reading. Change-point detection finds the day the behaviour changed.

NeedsMonths of history from L1/L2 features
CatchesSlow wear, early warning
EffortDays to set up; it pays off over time

7 · What to collect

Data checklist for each machine

  • Raw waveforms, not only RMS values. Sampling rate at least 2.5 × the highest frequency you care about (for bearings, 25 kHz or more is good).
  • A speed signal recorded at the same time: tachometer or once-per-turn mark (keyphasor). Every fault frequency is a multiple of speed.
  • Operating state for each recording: load, flow, pressure, temperature.
  • Geometry: bearing part numbers (or ball count and sizes), blade and vane counts, gear teeth if any.
  • Sensor details: type, mounting position, direction (radial or axial), and which unit it is on.
  • Maintenance log: dates of repairs, part changes and inspections. Even one past repair can be used as a real test case.
  • Healthy data across all speeds and loads the machine really uses, not only one operating point.

8 · What not to do

Mistakes we already paid for

  • Do not train a spectrogram network on lab data and expect it to work on your machine. 11 of 11 runs failed, in both directions, with 2 software versions.
  • Do not use domain adaptation (DANN) as a fix. It never beat the plain model and got worse with more data.
  • Do not trust one run. Six runs that differed only in the random seed gave fault recall from 0.42 to 0.97.
  • Do not tune anything on the faults you test on. Without fault data, you cannot measure recall on your own machine. Be suspicious of any method that claims to.
  • Do not judge a detector by accuracy or F1 alone. Always look at the false-alarm rate on healthy data. In Experiment 2, a "good" F1 came with 40–75% false alarms.

Words used in this report

Healthy-only / one-class
A detector trained only on normal data. It flags anything that looks different.
False alarm rate
Share of healthy windows wrongly flagged as faulty.
Recall
Share of real faults that the detector found.
Balanced accuracy
Average of recall and (1 − false alarm rate). 0.5 is guessing.
Envelope
The outline of a signal's loudness over time. It shows the rhythm of hits and hides the ringing tone.
Order, 1X, 2X
Frequency measured in multiples of shaft speed. 1X = once per turn. Orders stay the same when speed changes.
BPFO / BPFI
How often balls pass a point on the outer / inner race. Calculated from bearing geometry.
Spectrogram
A picture of which frequencies are present over time. Experiments 1 and 3 used it.
DANN
Domain-Adversarial Neural Network: a method that tries to make features look the same on two machines.
Calibration
Setting the alarm level from the target machine's own healthy data so the false-alarm rate is known.
