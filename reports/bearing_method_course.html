A course on method v1.1 · the bearing monitor

Bearing Method Course

You know the earlier method: envelope features and a logistic regression trained on CWRU. This course explains the method we use now, one idea at a time, starting from what you already know. The labs are live: they generate a bearing signal in your browser and run the real steps of the method on it, so you can move a slider and watch what changes.

The labs use simulated signals (a 6203 bearing, 12 kHz). Real datasets are cited as evidence at the end of each lesson.

Part A · The signal

LESSON 01

Why a damaged bearing knocks in rhythm

A ball bearing has an outer ring (outer race), an inner ring (inner race), balls, and a cage that keeps the balls apart. If one race has a crack or a pit, every ball that rolls over it makes a small impact. These impacts repeat at a rate that depends only on:

  • the shaft speed;
  • the bearing's shape: number of balls n, ball diameter d, pitch diameter D (the circle through the ball centres), and contact angle α.
r = (d / D) · cos(α) BPFO = (n / 2) · (1 − r) outer race: impacts per shaft turn BPFI = (n / 2) · (1 + r) inner race FTF = (1 / 2) · (1 − r) cage turns per shaft turn BSF = (D / 2d) · (1 − r²) ball spin check: BPFO + BPFI = n (always)

These are already "per shaft turn" numbers (orders, next lesson). Multiply by the shaft speed in Hz to get Hz.

Why does an inner-race fault give more impacts per turn than an outer-race fault?

The inner race turns with the shaft, so a point on it meets the balls more often than a point on the stationary outer race. That is why BPFI > BPFO, and they always add up to the number of balls.

LESSON 02

Orders: units that do not change with speed

An order is a frequency divided by the shaft speed (in Hz). The outer-race fault of a 6203 is 3.053 orders:

  • at 900 rpm (15 Hz) that is 45.8 Hz;
  • at 1500 rpm (25 Hz), 76.3 Hz.

Measured in Hz, the fault line moves every time the speed changes. In orders it stays at 3.053.

This is what lets one method work at any speed: we always read the spectrum in orders. It needs the shaft speed, which is why the method requires it.

Try itIn the signal lab below, move the speed slider with the x-axis in Hz, then switch to orders. In Hz the fault lines slide around; in orders they stand still on the dashed markers.
LESSON 03

The envelope: hear the rhythm, not the ring

A single impact is very short. It makes the bearing housing ring like a small bell, at a high frequency set by the structure (here about 3.3 kHz). So in the normal spectrum, a fault shows up as a bump around 3.3 kHz, which tells you nothing about the rhythm, and every machine rings at a different frequency.

The envelope follows the loudness of the ringing over time: a peak at each knock. Its spectrum therefore shows the knock rate, a clean line at BPFO or BPFI and its multiples (harmonics). The ringing frequency, which belongs to the machine and not to the fault, drops out. That is why envelope analysis can move between machines.

In practice, the envelope comes from the Hilbert transform: it turns the signal into a rotating arrow whose length is the envelope.

Why can't we just look for a peak at BPFO in the normal spectrum?

Because the knocks themselves are broadband pulses. The energy sits around the structure's ringing frequency, and the knock rhythm only appears as tiny, smeared sidebands there. The envelope turns that rhythm into a clear line.

LESSON 04

Pre-whitening: no frequency band to choose

Before

Band-pass 1.5–5.5 kHz, then the envelope. The band was a guess about where bearing resonances live. If the real resonance is outside it, or a strong machine tone sits inside it, the result suffers. At Paderborn 1500 rpm this was one suspect for the drop.

Now

Cepstrum pre-whitening (CPW): compute the spectrum, set every frequency's magnitude to 1 (keep only its phase), and transform back. Loud steady tones and the machine's resonance shape are flattened, while sharp knocks survive, because their energy is spread over all frequencies at once. Then the envelope is taken of the whole band.

X(f) = FFT(window) X_white(f) = X(f) / |X(f)| every frequency gets magnitude 1 x_white(t) = inverse FFT(X_white)

Nothing here is chosen or tuned, so it cannot be tuned to fault labels either. In the lab, switch to "Before" and see that the 2.2 kHz machine tone (outside the band) disappears, but so does any information outside 1.5–5.5 kHz. Pre-whitening flattens the tone while keeping the knocks.

LESSON 05

The squared envelope spectrum (SES)

After whitening we take the analytic signal (Hilbert transform), its squared magnitude (the envelope squared), remove its mean, apply a Hann window, and take the spectrum. Squaring makes impacts stand out more against background noise. This is the standard "CPW + SES" recipe from the bearing-diagnostics literature (Borghesani et al. 2013). The frequency axis is then divided by the shaft speed to get orders.

a(t) = x_white(t) + j · Hilbert(x_white)(t) e(t) = |a(t)|² − mean SES(f) = | FFT( e(t) · Hann(t) ) | order = f / shaft_Hz

Part B · Reading the spectrum

LESSON 06

Six fault lines (needs the bearing's geometry)

If we know the bearing, we know exactly where to look: BPFO and BPFI, and their 2nd and 3rd harmonics. For each of these six places we take the highest point within ±0.08 orders (to allow for a little slip and speed error), and divide it by the total of the envelope spectrum between 0.5 and 12 orders. Dividing makes the number independent of how loud the machine is or how sensitive the sensor is.

line(k) = max SES within (order_k ± 0.08) / sum of SES over 0.5–12 orders order_k ∈ { BPFO, 2·BPFO, 3·BPFO, BPFI, 2·BPFI, 3·BPFI }

This was the main detector until method v1.0. It is still used for typing (lesson 16), because only named lines can say "outer" or "inner".

LESSON 07

Geometry-free: 135 bins instead of six lines

This is the idea behind the name. "Geometry-free" means the detector does not need to know the bearing's geometry, that is, its number of balls and its diameters. Instead of reading six exact places, it reads the whole stretch of the envelope spectrum where bearing faults can appear:

orders 1.5 → 15, cut into 135 bins of width 0.1 order bin(j) = max SES inside bin j / sum of SES over 0.5–12 orders (same normalisation as the lines)

Why 1.5 to 15 orders?

For ordinary ball bearings, BPFO is about 0.4 × (number of balls) and BPFI about 0.6 × (number of balls). With 7 to 20 balls, the fault fundamentals fall roughly between 2.5 and 12 orders, and their harmonics continue above. Starting at 1.5 skips shaft rotation (1 order), which every machine has whether healthy or not. 15 keeps the range inside the analysis bandwidth at normal speeds.

How can it detect a fault without knowing where to look?

Because the detection step (lessons 9–10) does not ask "is BPFO high?". It asks "did any bin rise above where it normally is on this machine?". A fault adds a new rhythm, and that rhythm lands in some bins: the fundamental, its harmonics, and the sidebands. Those bins rise against their own healthy history. We do not need to know in advance which bins they will be.

What it gains
  • Works for any bearing, even an unknown one.
  • Catches energy the six lines miss: higher harmonics, sidebands, the other race, partly ball damage.
  • Measured: as good as or better than the six lines on 6 of 6 development datasets, on HUST (4/5 sizes), on LDM, and on IMS.
What it costs
  • It cannot say which race. It reports the "dominant order" (the bin that rose most), and you need the geometry to interpret it.
  • Looking at 135 places gives more chances for a healthy bin to wobble high, so its threshold needs more healthy data to be set well (lesson 11).

Because geometry-free beat the six lines on both pre-registered comparisons on unseen data (HUST and LDM), it became the main detector in method v1.1. The six lines stay for typing whenever the geometry is known.

A bearing's BPFO is 3.05 orders. Which bins will rise for an outer-race fault?

Mainly the bins around 3.0–3.1 (fundamental), 6.1 (2×), 9.1–9.2 (3×), and 12.2 (4×). The detector does not need to know this: it will see those bins rise against their healthy values. In the lab, the green highlight shows the bin that rose most above 12 healthy windows of the same simulated machine. On a healthy signal the largest z is still around 2–3: that is just normal wobble.

Part C · The decision

LESSON 08

No classifier: compare the machine with itself

Before

A logistic regression learned "fault vs healthy" from CWRU: one rig, one bearing type, large artificial faults, about 1,750 rpm. On a different machine its decision boundary sat in the wrong place. At Paderborn 1500 rpm recall fell to 0.29–0.51, and on Ottawa 2018 AUC was 0.48, a coin toss.

Now

No learning from faults at all. For each unit we learn only what its own healthy envelope spectrum looks like. A fault is anything that rises clearly above that. Nothing from another machine is carried over except the physics of how to compute the spectrum.

This removes the reason the old method failed across machines and speeds: there is no learned boundary to put in the wrong place. The price is that every unit needs healthy recordings of itself before monitoring starts.

LESSON 09

The healthy baseline and the z-score

Take a unit's healthy recordings and cut them into 1 s windows. For each bin (or line), take the logarithm of its value in every healthy window and compute the mean and standard deviation. Together they are the unit's baseline: "where this bin normally sits, and how much it normally wobbles".

For a new window, each bin's z-score is:

z(bin) = ( log(value) − mean_log(bin) ) / sd_log(bin)
  • Why the log? Spectrum values are always positive and spread over a wide range. In log form, "twice as high" is the same step everywhere, and the wobble looks like an ordinary bell curve.
  • What does z mean? How many "normal wobbles" above usual. z = 3 means three standard deviations higher than this unit ever usually goes. Only rises count; a bin going down is not a fault sign.
LESSON 10

Take the largest rise

A window now has 135 z-scores, or 6 for the line detector. Its score is the largest of them:

score(window) = max over bins of z(bin)

Why the maximum and not the average? A fault usually lights up a few bins, not all of them. Averaging 135 bins would bury two strong ones under 133 normal ones. We tested averaging (feature set v2, "family mean") and it was worse. The maximum catches the single bin that jumped.

The maximum has a side effect: even on healthy data, the largest of 135 wobbling numbers is usually around 2–3, not 0. So we never use a fixed rule like "z > 3". The threshold is learned from the healthy scores themselves (next lesson).

LESSON 11

The conformal threshold

Score every healthy calibration window. Sort the scores. With n scores and a target false-alarm rate α = 10%, the threshold is the ⌈(n+1)·0.9⌉-th smallest score.

threshold = sorted_healthy_scores[ ceil((n + 1) · (1 − α)) ] α = 0.10

The promise of this "conformal" rule: if a new healthy window behaves like the calibration windows, the chance it exceeds the threshold is at most 10%. No bell-curve assumption is needed; it only counts. With fewer than 9 windows the rule cannot reach 90% and the threshold is infinite, so the service refuses to calibrate.

So a "flagged" window means: higher than about 90% of this unit's healthy windows. On its own a flagged window is weak evidence. Many flagged windows in a recording is strong evidence (lesson 15).

LESSON 12

Cross-fitting: don't let the baseline grade itself

There is a trap. The healthy windows that set the mean and sd are the same windows we then score to set the threshold. Each window helped define "normal", so it looks more normal than a new healthy window would. The threshold comes out too low, and on new healthy data more than 10% of windows get flagged.

Cross-fitting fixes this: split the baseline into groups (one per recording, or three time blocks if there is only one recording). Score each group with a baseline built from the other groups only, then set the threshold on those honest scores.

LESSON 13

Own baseline vs fleet baseline

Could we use healthy data from other units of the same type as the baseline? It is tempting, especially for a new machine. The data says no:

  • On Ottawa 2023, comparing each bearing with the other 19 bearings' healthy recordings gave AUC 0.71, and one healthy bearing was flagged on every window.
  • Comparing each bearing with its own healthy recording gave AUC 0.93.

Every unit has its own normal: its mounting, its sensor position, its tolerances, small differences in the bearing. A "fleet" baseline mixes those differences into the noise, so faults hide in it and odd healthy units look faulty. That is why the product calibrates one baseline per unit. It is also why the most common failure we saw everywhere was "one unit whose healthy data did not look like its own other healthy data" (Paderborn K005, Ottawa 2023 b02, HUST 6205).

LESSON 15

From windows to a recording verdict

A recording of 10 s gives about 19 one-second windows (they overlap by half). We count the share that are over the threshold:

Share of windows flaggedVerdictWhy these numbers
< 40%healthyAbout 10% of healthy windows are flagged by design, so a healthy recording usually lands near 10%.
≥ 40%watchMeasured on held-out healthy Paderborn units: at 25%, too many healthy recordings (23%) reached watch; at 40%, about 12%.
≥ 50%faultyAbout 5% of healthy recordings reach this. Most real damage goes well above.

On IMS, the failing bearings ended with 88–97% of windows flagged; healthy-life recordings sat around 10–20%.

LESSON 16

Typing hint: which race?

When the geometry is known, the service also keeps a baseline of the six named lines. On the windows the detector flagged, it compares the largest z among the three outer-race lines with the largest among the three inner-race lines. Whichever rose more is the hint, reported as "outer race (hint)" or "inner race (hint)", along with the single line that rose most, e.g. bpfo_h2 z=6.1.

TestRight race
XJTU-SY, real run-to-failure damage10 of 11
HUST, 5 bearing sizes83% of windows
Paderborn (development)74–81%
IMS final exam2–3 of 3
LDM, race + ball damage together7 of 11

Good, but not reliable enough to call a diagnosis, especially when two kinds of damage exist at once. Hence "hint". Ball and cage faults are not covered by this step.

Part D · Over time

LESSON 17

The level and the CUSUM trend alarm

The detector judges one recording at a time. Damage usually grows, so it also helps to watch the trend across recordings. Two series are kept per unit:

  • Level: the log of the vibration RMS of each recording, as a z-score against the healthy recordings. When speed and load vary (and are logged), the level is first adjusted for them. A straight-line fit, learned on the healthy recordings, says what log RMS to expect at this speed and load, and we use the difference (the residual). On LDM this cut false alarms from 0.19 to 0.15 and gave the most detections (14 of 17).
  • Physics: the mean window score of the recording, standardised the same way.

Each series feeds a CUSUM:

C₀ = 0 C_t = max( 0 , C_{t−1} + z_t − k ) k = 0.5 alarm when C_t > h h ≥ 5, set from the healthy recordings

In words: each recording adds how far it is above normal, minus a small allowance k. Normal recordings (z around 0) drain the sum back towards 0. A steady rise keeps adding until the sum crosses h. A single noisy recording is not enough.

LESSON 18

When the speed changes inside a recording: order tracking

Everything above assumes the speed is constant during one 1 s window. On some machines it is not: in Ottawa 2018 it sweeps from 13 to 28 Hz within 10 s. Then a fault at 3.57 orders smears across many Hz, and the line blurs.

Computed order tracking fixes this with an encoder or tachometer that tells the shaft angle at every moment:

  1. compute the envelope in time, as usual (the ringing is in Hz);
  2. resample the envelope to equal steps of shaft angle (128 samples per revolution) instead of equal steps of time;
  3. take the spectrum over 20 revolutions. Its axis is now directly in orders, and the fault line is sharp again.
Ottawa 2018 (speed sweeping 13–28 Hz)AUC
Encoder + order tracking0.922
Encoder, but one average speed per window0.804
Speed estimated from the vibration itself0.661

This is why the method insists on a measured speed signal.

LESSON 20

Requirements, limits, and the evidence

What a unit needs

  • Raw vibration at ≥ 25 kHz, recordings ≥ 1 s long.
  • Shaft speed for every recording: a tachometer or encoder, or the setpoint of a speed-controlled drive.
  • At least 30 minutes of its own healthy data, covering all its operating states.
  • Optional but valuable: the bearing designation or geometry, for typing.

What it does not do

  • Ball and cage faults: mostly missed.
  • A brand-new unit with no healthy history of its own.
  • Reliable multi-day trend alarms: the CUSUM drifts (lesson 17).

Evidence for each design choice

FINAL SELF-TEST

Ten questions

1. In one sentence, what does "geometry-free" mean?

The detector watches 135 small slices of the envelope spectrum (1.5–15 orders) against the unit's own healthy history, so it does not need the bearing's ball count or diameters.

2. Why do we divide frequency by shaft speed?

To get orders, which do not change with speed. A fault line then sits at the same place at 900 or 1500 rpm.

3. What problem does pre-whitening solve that the old 1.5–5.5 kHz band did not?

It removes the machine's own resonance shape and steady tones without anyone choosing a band, so nothing is guessed or tuned.

4. Why did the old method fail at 1500 rpm on Paderborn?

Its decision came from a classifier trained on CWRU; that boundary did not fit Paderborn at 1500 rpm. The physics lines themselves still carried the information (AUC 0.84–0.86).

5. A bin's healthy log values have mean −6.0 and sd 0.5. A new window has log value −4.5. What is its z?

(−4.5 − (−6.0)) / 0.5 = 3.0, three normal wobbles above usual.

6. Why can't we just alarm when the largest z exceeds 3?

With 135 bins, the largest healthy z is often already 2–3. The threshold must be learned from healthy maximum scores (the conformal rule).

7. With 40 healthy scores and α = 10%, which score is the threshold?

ceil(41 × 0.9) = ceil(36.9) = the 37th smallest score.

8. What goes wrong without cross-fitting, and when does it matter?

The baseline judges its own windows too kindly, so the threshold is too low and false alarms exceed 10%. It matters with small baselines; with thousands of windows it makes no difference.

9. When does the service say "outer race (hint)" and when does it say nothing?

When flagged windows exist and the geometry is known, it compares the outer and inner lines' largest z. With unknown geometry it reports only the dominant order.

10. What failed in the final exam, and what held?

The detection score held (it rose on all 4 failing bearings). The trend alarm failed: it fired on healthy bearings too, about once per day.

GLOSSARY

Words used in this course

Order
A frequency divided by shaft speed in Hz; "per shaft turn".
BPFO / BPFI
Ball-pass frequency of the outer / inner race: impacts per shaft turn when that race is damaged.
BSF / FTF
Ball spin frequency / cage (fundamental train) frequency.
Harmonic
A whole multiple of a frequency (2×, 3× …). A train of sharp knocks has many.
Envelope
The loudness curve of the vibration over time. Its spectrum shows how often knocks repeat.
Pre-whitening (CPW)
Setting every frequency's magnitude to 1 before the envelope, removing resonance shape and steady tones.
SES
Squared envelope spectrum: the spectrum of the squared envelope.
Bin
One 0.1-order slice of the envelope spectrum. Its value is the highest point inside it, divided by the spectrum's total.
Geometry-free
Detection from the 135 bins; needs no bearing geometry, gives no race.
Baseline
A unit's healthy windows: the mean and spread of each bin's log value.
z-score
How many standard deviations above the baseline mean a value is.
Conformal threshold
The healthy score below which 90% of healthy windows fall, with a guarantee on new healthy windows.
Cross-fitting
Scoring each part of the baseline with a baseline built without it, so the threshold is honest.
Fleet baseline
Using other units' healthy data. Measured to be much worse than a unit's own.
CUSUM
A running sum of how far each recording is above normal minus an allowance; alarms on sustained rises.
Order tracking
Resampling to equal steps of shaft angle so orders stay sharp when speed changes.
